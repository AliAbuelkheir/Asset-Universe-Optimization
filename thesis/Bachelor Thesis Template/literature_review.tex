\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:litreview}

\section[Framing the Problem]{Framing the Problem}

This thesis studies asset-universe selection as a stage that comes before final portfolio weighting. An optimizer can only allocate among assets that are admitted into the candidate set. If that set contains assets that are unsuitable for the investor's risk tolerance, then the allocation stage begins from a weak starting point. For this reason, the literature reviewed in this chapter includes classical portfolio optimization, AI/ML asset preselection, investor-risk personalization, and adaptive reinforcement-learning methods.

The review is organized around one central distinction. Many existing papers already show that selecting assets before portfolio optimization can improve later portfolio construction. However, the criterion used to define a suitable asset is often expected return, price movement, profitability, Sharpe-like performance, efficiency, robustness, or downstream optimizer quality. This thesis defines suitability differently. It studies whether assets can be selected for conservative, balanced, and aggressive investor groups by directly predicting asset-level realized risk before allocation.

\section[Portfolio Optimization and the Fixed-Universe Assumption]{Portfolio Optimization and the Fixed-Universe Assumption}

A classical starting point is the mean-variance framework introduced by Markowitz \cite{markowitz1952}. The framework formalizes the tradeoff between return and risk when assigning portfolio weights, but it assumes that the investable universe is already known. This assumption is important for the present thesis because it separates two decisions that are often combined in practice: choosing the candidate assets and assigning final weights.

Later allocation methods provide additional ways to represent investor views and risk objectives. The Black-Litterman model incorporates investor views into global allocation decisions \cite{blacklitterman1992}, while conditional value-at-risk optimization focuses on downside tail risk \cite{rockafellar2000optimization}. Quantile-based portfolio selection also extends the discussion beyond variance by studying distributional risk criteria \cite{bodnar2021quantile}. These approaches are relevant because they show that risk can be modeled in several ways, but they still mainly operate after the asset universe has been defined.

Benchmarking work also highlights why the universe-selection stage should be separated from the weighting stage. DeMiguel et al. \cite{demiguel2009optimal} showed that naive diversification is a difficult benchmark for optimized portfolios to beat, while hierarchical risk parity later offered a risk-based allocation alternative that emphasizes diversification structure \cite{lopezdeprado2016building}. These methods are useful baselines for allocation, but they do not decide which assets are suitable for a specific investor before weights are computed.

\section[AI/ML Asset Preselection Before Optimization]{AI/ML Asset Preselection Before Optimization}

The closest baseline family to this thesis is AI/ML-based asset preselection before portfolio optimization. A deep-learning preselection framework was proposed by Wang et al. \cite{wang2020portfolioformation}, where selected assets are passed to downstream portfolio formation. This paper is directly relevant because it treats asset filtering as a meaningful stage before allocation. Its selection logic, however, is not based on direct prediction of an asset-level realized-risk target for investor risk-tolerance grouping.

Similar predict-then-select patterns appear in later studies. Ma et al. \cite{ma2021returnprediction} connected machine-learning and deep-learning return prediction to portfolio optimization. Kaczmarek and Perez \cite{kaczmarek2022building} used machine-learning predictions to build portfolios under rules such as mean-variance optimization, hierarchical risk parity, and naive diversification. Chaweewanchon and Chaysiri \cite{chaweewanchon2022} used predictive stock selection before Markowitz optimization. These studies show that ML can serve as a filter before final allocation, but the filtering signal is mainly return or price-prediction driven.

Other work broadens preselection beyond simple return prediction. Mills and Anyomi \cite{mills2022twostage} proposed a two-stage robust portfolio construction approach under uncertainty. Hosseinzadeh et al. \cite{hosseinzadeh2023dea} used data envelopment analysis for asset preselection before portfolio optimization. Abdi et al. \cite{abdi2024prospective} combined LSTM modeling with Sharpe-ratio maximization for prospective asset preselection. Chou and Pham \cite{chou2025ensemble} used ensemble learning for stock preselection with multiobjective investment optimization and investor-preference framing.

Taken together, these papers establish that preselection before allocation is a valid modeling stage. They also form the main baseline family for this thesis. The key difference is the definition of suitability. In most of this literature, a suitable asset is one expected to improve return, price prediction, efficiency, Sharpe-like performance, robustness, or downstream optimizer quality. In this thesis, a suitable asset is one whose predicted realized-risk behavior matches the investor's risk-tolerance group before any allocation model is applied.

\section[Investor Risk Tolerance and Personalization]{Investor Risk Tolerance and Personalization}

The preselection literature motivates the stage at which this thesis operates, but investor-specific suitability requires a second body of work. Personalized finance advisory systems show that recommendations can change when investor information is modeled explicitly. Musto et al. \cite{musto2015personalized} used case-based recommendation and diversification strategies for personalized finance advice. Although the output is not a formal asset universe, the paper supports the principle that financial recommendation should not treat all investors as identical.

Recent AI-based personalization studies provide additional support for investor-aware financial decisions. Asemi et al. \cite{asemi2023demographics} used an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system to customize investment type from investor demographics and feedback. A related model combined ANFIS, clustering, expert feedback, and multimodal neural-network pretraining for investment-type recommendation \cite{asemi2024investmenttype}. Wei and Liu \cite{wei2025fundrecommendation} proposed personalized fund recommendation with dynamic utility learning. These papers connect investor information to financial recommendations, but their outputs are usually investment types or fund recommendations rather than an asset-level universe before allocation.

Risk tolerance is also studied directly in robo-advising and personalized portfolio design. Alsabah et al. \cite{alsabah2021roboadvising} modeled how investor risk preferences can be learned from portfolio choices. Yu and Liu \cite{yu2021personalized} proposed a personalized mean-CVaR portfolio optimization model for individual investment. Capponi et al. \cite{capponi2022personalizedrobo} studied personalized robo-advising through client interaction. Schneider and Yilmaz \cite{schneider2025riskappetite} showed that risk appetite can change stock and portfolio recommendations produced by ChatGPT. These studies justify the use of investor risk tolerance as a decision variable, but they do not generally define an explicit pre-allocation asset universe selected through direct asset-risk prediction.

\section[Adaptive AI and Reinforcement Learning in Finance]{Adaptive AI and Reinforcement Learning in Finance}

Adaptive AI methods are relevant because financial suitability is not static. Deep reinforcement learning has been used for trading and portfolio-management tasks, including direct financial signal representation and trading decisions \cite{deng2017deepdirect}. Jiang et al. \cite{jiang2017portfolioframework} proposed a deep reinforcement-learning framework for portfolio management, and FinRL later provided environments and tools for automated stock trading and quantitative finance research \cite{liu2020finrl}.

Additional DRL portfolio studies show how adaptive policies can be evaluated in financial settings. Lucarelli and Borrotti \cite{lucarelli2020deepq} studied deep Q-learning for cryptocurrency portfolio management. Soleymani and Paquet \cite{soleymani2020deepbreath} used online deep reinforcement learning with an autoencoder representation for portfolio optimization. Pinelis and Ruppert \cite{pinelis2022machineallocation} studied machine-learning portfolio allocation, while Rezaei and Nezamabadi-Pour \cite{rezaei2025taxonomy} provided a taxonomy and experimental study of deep reinforcement learning in portfolio management.

Risk-aware reinforcement learning is especially relevant to the implementation direction of this thesis. Choudhary et al. \cite{choudhary2025riskadjusted} trained risk-adjusted deep reinforcement-learning agents using reward functions related to return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. This supports the use of RL in risk-aware financial modeling. However, the action in such work is still usually a trading or allocation decision, not the prediction of an asset-level risk score used to form a pre-allocation universe.

The closest paper to the present thesis is the investor-specific DRL framework of Orra et al. \cite{orra2025deep}. Their pipeline uses volatility-guided grouping to form conservative, moderate, and aggressive risk profiles before DRL allocation. This is highly relevant because it connects investor risk profiles, asset grouping, and adaptive learning. The remaining difference is that the grouping is volatility-guided and stock-focused, while this thesis predicts a composite realized-risk target based on realized volatility, downside deviation, and maximum drawdown over a mixed and variable asset universe.

\section[Synthesis and Research Gap]{Synthesis and Research Gap}

The reviewed literature leads to three observations. First, classical allocation methods provide strong tools for assigning portfolio weights, but they usually assume that the investable universe has already been chosen. Second, AI/ML preselection studies show that selecting assets before optimization is a valid modeling stage. Third, investor-personalization studies show that risk tolerance should affect financial recommendations. The gap appears between these streams rather than inside only one of them.

The main baseline family for this thesis is AI/ML asset preselection before portfolio optimization. These studies solve a related problem and should not be dismissed. Their limitation is narrower: the selection criterion is usually return prediction, price movement, profitability, Sharpe-like performance, efficiency, robustness, or downstream optimizer quality. Direct prediction of an asset-level risk target is mostly absent. The closest partial exception is Orra et al. \cite{orra2025deep}, which uses volatility for risk-profile grouping, but it does not predict a composite realized-risk target in the same form used here.

This thesis is positioned as an investor-risk-tolerance-oriented extension of the preselection literature. It studies pre-allocation universe construction by predicting and ranking assets according to a composite \texttt{realized\_risk} target before final allocation. The selected universes are then interpreted according to conservative, balanced, and aggressive investor profiles. In this way, the thesis connects asset preselection, investor risk tolerance, and adaptive AI while keeping final portfolio weighting as a downstream stage.
